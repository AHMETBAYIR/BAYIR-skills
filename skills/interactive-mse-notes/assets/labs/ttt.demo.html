A TTT diagram answers “what happens if I hold this steel at a temperature?” A CCT diagram answers the question a heat treater actually asks: “what happens if I cool it?” This note builds one eutectoid steel (0.76 wt% C), follows a cooling path across both diagrams, and turns the crossings into a microstructure and a hardness number.

What you should be able to do

  • Read a TTT diagram: A1 = 727 °C, Ms = 220 °C, and the C-shaped start and finish curves.
  • Predict whether a given cooling rate forms pearlite, bainite, martensite, or a mixture.
  • Explain why the CCT curves sit to the right of and below the TTT curves.
  • Check a microstructure by making the fractions sum to one and by estimating HV.

A map of what the steel does while it cools

A time–temperature–transformation (TTT) diagram is an isothermal map. Each curve is measured by holding a small specimen at one temperature and watching how long the transformation takes. The horizontal axis is time on a logarithmic scale; the vertical axis is temperature. The two C-shaped curves that bound the transformation region are the start curve (1% transformed) and the finish curve (99% transformed).

Definition — TTT and CCT. A TTT diagram gives the start and finish times of an isothermal transformation at each temperature. A continuous-cooling-transformation (CCT) diagram gives the temperatures at which the same transformation starts and finishes when the specimen is cooled at a constant rate. They are different experiments, so they are different diagrams.

For the eutectoid composition, three products compete:

  • Pearlite — alternating ferrite and cementite lamellae, formed at high undercooling just below A1. Its nose is near 550 °C at about 1 s, so it is the easiest product to form.
  • Bainite — a finer ferrite–carbide aggregate formed at lower temperature. Its nose is near 350 °C at about 10 s, so it needs more time at temperature.
  • Martensite — a diffusionless shear transformation that begins at Ms = 220 °C. It needs no incubation time; it needs only that the austenite survives that far.

The nose is the point closest to the time axis. It is the shortest time at which the transformation can start, and it therefore controls how fast you may cool and still transform. A constant cooling rate r from 800 °C passes the nose temperature Tnose at t = (800 − Tnose)/r. Setting that time equal to the nose time gives the critical cooling rate:

$$r_{\text{crit}} \approx \frac{800 - T_{\text{nose}}}{t_{\text{nose}}}$$

For the CCT pearlite nose at 525 °C and 10 s this estimate gives (800 − 525)/10 = 27.5 °C/s. The lab finds the same value by walking the path against the curve, because the nose is the first contact point for these curves. Cool faster than rcrit and the path misses the start curve; the austenite then reaches Ms and becomes martensite.

From path crossings to fractions

The model uses three rules, in order. First, if the path enters the pearlite start curve, pearlite forms; how much depends on how far the path penetrates toward the pearlite finish. Second, if it then enters the bainite start curve, bainite forms from the austenite that pearlite left behind. Third, whatever austenite is left when the path reaches Ms = 220 °C transforms to martensite, except for a retained fraction of 10% of that austenite. The four fractions are then normalised so that they sum to exactly one.

The fractions are not a kinetic calculation. They are a monotone estimate: deeper penetration of the start–finish band means more of that constituent. That is enough to teach the competition, but it does not reproduce a measured CCT diagram.

Worked example — CCT, 10 °C/s, no hold, 1 kg of austenite.

(1) Time to 500 °C: (800 − 500)/10 = 30 s. (2) The path crosses the pearlite start near 584 °C and the bainite start near 398 °C. (3) The model returns 0.48 kg pearlite, 0.22 kg bainite, 0.27 kg martensite, and 0.03 kg retained austenite. Mass balance: 0.48 + 0.22 + 0.27 + 0.03 = 1.00 kg, so no mass is lost or invented. (4) Weighted hardness: 0.48(250) + 0.22(350) + 0.27(700) + 0.03(200) = 120 + 77 + 189 + 6 = 392 HV.

The 10% retained-austenite rule applies only to the austenite still present at Ms. Here that is 0.30 kg, so 0.03 kg is retained and 0.27 kg becomes martensite. If the path had formed 100% pearlite above Ms, there would be no austenite left and therefore no retained austenite.

Check the units: kilograms in, kilograms out; volume fractions are treated as mass fractions in this schematic; HV is a weighted average of hardness values, not a rule of mixtures for strength. The hardness numbers are approximate because real pearlite spacing, bainite morphology, and carbon in solution all move them.

Why the CCT curves move right and down

An isothermal experiment gives the steel unlimited time at one temperature. Continuous cooling gives it only a short visit to each temperature, so the transformation is delayed: the start curve moves to longer times (right) and to lower temperatures (down). The finish curve moves with it. The shift is not a change in the steel; it is a change in the experiment.

In this model the CCT pearlite nose moves from 550 °C / 1 s to 525 °C / 10 s, and its critical rate falls from about 250 °C/s to about 27 °C/s. That is why a steel that would transform isothermally at 550 °C can miss the transformation entirely when cooled quickly. The bainite curve shifts less, because bainite is already sluggish and needs a long hold even in the isothermal experiment.

Common mistakes

  • “Crossing the start curve gives 100% of that phase.” The start curve is 1% transformed. The fraction grows as the path moves toward the finish curve. A path that clips the start and then leaves the band gives only a little pearlite, not a fully pearlitic structure.
  • “A constant cooling rate is a straight line on this plot.” It is straight on temperature versus linear time. The axis here is logarithmic, so the same path curves: equal temperature drops take equal times, but each equal time step covers fewer decades as time grows.
  • “Faster cooling always gives a harder part.” Past the critical rate the structure is already martensite plus retained austenite. In this model, 100 °C/s and 200 °C/s both give 90% martensite and 10% retained austenite, so both give 650 HV: extra cooling speed cannot remove the retained austenite. Real steels can also crack or distort when cooled this fast.
  • “TTT and CCT are two views of the same curves.” They are different experiments. Using an isothermal start curve to judge a continuous cool overestimates how much transformation occurs, because the path does not spend the isothermal time at any one temperature.

Summary and exercises

A TTT diagram is an isothermal map; a CCT diagram is a continuous-cooling map. The nose controls the critical cooling rate, and the CCT nose sits right and lower, so the critical rate is lower. Cross the start curve to begin a transformation; approach the finish curve to complete it; whatever austenite reaches Ms becomes martensite, with some retained austenite. Check every microstructure by making the fractions sum to one.

1. A eutectoid steel is cooled at 2 °C/s and at 50 °C/s. Compute the time to reach 500 °C in each case. Which path is more likely to cross the pearlite start in the CCT view, and why?

Solution

At 2 °C/s: (800 − 500)/2 = 150 s. At 50 °C/s: (800 − 500)/50 = 6 s. The slow path spends far longer in the 500–600 °C window, so it sits to the right of the start curve and crosses it; the fast path is still far to the left and misses. The start curve is a time threshold, not a temperature threshold.

2. A microstructure contains 60% pearlite, 25% bainite, 12% martensite, and 3% retained austenite. Estimate HV with the lab's values (250, 350, 700, 200). Check the fractions first.

Solution

The fractions sum to 0.60 + 0.25 + 0.12 + 0.03 = 1.00. HV = 0.60(250) + 0.25(350) + 0.12(700) + 0.03(200) = 150 + 87.5 + 84 + 6 = 327.5 ≈ 328 HV. The result is dominated by the martensite despite its small fraction, because its hardness is roughly three times that of pearlite.

3. Explain why a 300 s hold at 620 °C can produce a fully pearlitic structure when the same steel cooled at 20 °C/s without a hold gives only about 15% pearlite.

Solution

Without the hold, the path passes 620 °C quickly and leaves the pearlite band before much transformation accumulates. The hold adds time at constant temperature, moving the path horizontally to the right; it crosses the start curve and then the finish curve, so the pearlite reaction can complete. The hold does not change A1, Ms, or the composition — it changes the time available.
