A property chart is a map, not a decision. The decision comes from what the part must do, what it must not do, and what you are trying to minimise. This page turns the Ashby chart into that argument: choose a property pair, change the exponent, move the constant-index line, and watch the candidate list change.

After this page you can

  • read a log–log property chart without treating the axes as linear;
  • derive the slope of a constant performance index on that chart;
  • separate a material property from a design constraint and an objective;
  • explain why a high-index material can still fail the shortlist.

Selection is not a property

A material property is a number with units. A performance index is a combination of properties that measures how well a material performs a function. For a light stiff tie, the mass is proportional to \(ρ/E\), so the index to maximise is \(E/ρ\). For a light stiff panel in bending, the thickness needed falls as \(E^{-1/3}\), so the index becomes \(E^{1/3}/\rho\). The exponent is not decoration; it encodes the load case.

Definition. For a property \(P\) and a density \(ρ\), a one-property performance index has the form

$$M=\frac{P^{a}}{\rho}$$
(1)

with \(a=1\) for a tie, \(a=1/2\) for a beam, and \(a=1/3\) for a panel in bending (stiffness-limited design). When the objective is cost instead of mass, replace \(ρ\) by cost per kilogram.

Taking logarithms of equation (1) gives a straight line on a log–log chart:

$$\log P=\frac{1}{a}\log\rho+\frac{1}{a}\log M$$
(2)

The slope is \(1/a\). A constant-index line is therefore a geometric statement about the index, not a trend fitted to the data. Move the line and you change the threshold, not the materials.

Where the performance index comes from

A design problem has three parts, and only the first is visible in the chart:

The three inputs to a materials index
InputQuestionExample: light stiff panel
FunctionWhat must the part do?Carry a bending load with a specified stiffness.
ConstraintWhat must it not do?Stay below a density limit; do not fracture; fit the space.
ObjectiveWhat is being minimised or maximised?Minimise mass.

For a panel of fixed area \(A\) and span \(L\), the bending stiffness scales as \(S \propto E t^3/L^3\), while the mass is \(m = \rho A t\). Eliminate the thickness \(t\) at fixed stiffness:

$$t \propto \left(\frac{S L^3}{E}\right)^{1/3},\qquad m \propto \rho\left(\frac{1}{E}\right)^{1/3}\quad\Rightarrow\quad m \propto \frac{\rho}{E^{1/3}}$$
(3)

To make the mass as small as possible, maximise \(E^{1/3}/\rho\). That is equation (1) with \(a=1/3\). A tie gives \(E/\rho\) and a beam with a free cross-section gives \(E^{1/2}/\rho\). The exponent is the engineering content; the log–log line is only its picture.

Do not read the winner off the chart. The chart shows \(E\) and \(ρ\). The slope \(1/a\) depends on the load case, and the constraint sliders decide which points are even allowed. A material can be far above the line and still be unacceptable because it is too dense, too weak, too costly, or too brittle.

Common mistakes

“The chart picks the material.” A chart is a property map. The index, the constraints, and the objective pick the material. Change the exponent from 1 to 1/3 and the ranking can change even though no data point moved.

“Higher modulus always wins.” Tungsten has \(E=411\) GPa but \(\rho=19.3\) g/cm³, so \(E^{1/3}/\rho\approx0.39\). Aluminium 6061 gives about 1.52 at one seventh of the density. The index is a ratio, not a league table of stiffness.

“The strength axis is the same for every family.” The dataset uses approximate yield strength for most metals and polymers, but tensile or flexural strength for cast iron, ceramics, and concrete. Ceramics fail from flaws and need Weibull statistics; a single strength number is a placeholder, not a design allowable.

“Cost and density are interchangeable.” The E vs cost chart uses cost in the denominator, so its index is stiffness per unit cost. Do not use that line to choose a light part, and do not compare its numerical \(M\) with the density-chart value; the denominators have different units. Concrete can win the raw stiffness-per-cost index because it is cheap and stiff in compression, but it is not a tensile structural material; the chart does not encode that failure mode.

Retrieval practice

Summary

  • A property chart plots material data; a performance index encodes the function, constraint, and objective.
  • For \(M=P^{a}/\rho\), constant-index lines on log–log axes have slope \(1/a\).
  • Common stiffness exponents are 1 (tie), 1/2 (beam), and 1/3 (panel).
  • Constraints can remove a high-index material; always report the candidate set, not only the winner.
  • Teaching data are approximate. Verify modulus, strength, cost, and failure mode before design.

Data and limits. The 28-material dataset is approximate teaching data after Ashby, Callister, and Granta EduPack-style property charts. Costs are order-of-magnitude material prices, not quotes. Strength values mix yield, tensile, and flexural measures by family; CFRP and wood values are along the fibre/grain direction. The chart does not model anisotropy, temperature, corrosion, fatigue, or processing. Verify before design.
