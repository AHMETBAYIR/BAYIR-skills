A band diagram is an energy map, not a picture of electrons. The gap between the valence and conduction bands is a property of the crystal; the Fermi level is a property of the doping and the temperature. This lab keeps the gap fixed and lets you move the Fermi level across seven decades of doping and a 200–500 K temperature window.

What you should be able to do

  • Place Ec, Ev, Ei, and EF on the same energy axis and read the offset in eV.
  • Predict which way EF moves when you add donors or acceptors.
  • Explain why heating an extrinsic semiconductor pulls EF back toward midgap.
  • Use np = ni² to check a majority/minority pair.

The gap is fixed; the Fermi level is not

The valence band is the sea of bonding states; the conduction band is the empty set of antibonding states above it. Their separation is the band gap Eg. At T = 0 K the valence band is full and the conduction band is empty. At finite temperature a few electrons are thermally promoted, leaving holes behind, and the electron and hole populations are tied together by

$$n_i = \sqrt{N_c N_v}\;e^{-E_g/2k_BT}$$

with Nc and Nv the effective densities of states at the band edges and kB = 8.617333 × 10−5 eV/K. The intrinsic level Ei is the chemical potential of the undoped crystal. Adding donors or acceptors moves EF away from Ei; it does not change Eg in this model.

How to read the number

For a non-degenerate n-type sample with full ionisation, n ≈ Nd. The Boltzmann relation n = ni exp[(EF − Ei)/kBT] then gives

$$E_F - E_i = k_BT\ln\!\left(\frac{N_d}{n_i}\right)$$

and the minority holes follow from np = ni². For p-type doping the sign flips: Ei − EF = kBT ln(Na/ni). The logarithm is why a factor-of-ten change in doping shifts EF by only 2.3 kBT, about 0.06 eV at room temperature.

Worked example — Si, Nd = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, 300 K.

(1) Densities of states: Nc = 2.8 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ and Nv = 1.04 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, so √(NcNv) = 1.71 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³. (2) Thermal factor: exp[−1.12/(2 × 0.025852)] = exp(−21.66) = 3.9 × 10⁻¹⁰. (3) ni = 1.71 × 10¹⁹ × 3.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 6.7 × 10⁹ cm⁻³. (4) Offset: kBT = 0.025852 eV, ln(10¹⁶/6.7 × 10⁹) = 14.2, so EF − Ei = 0.37 eV. (5) Minority holes: p = ni²/n = (6.7 × 10⁹)²/10¹⁶ = 4.5 × 10³ cm⁻³. The product np = 4.5 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻⁶ is ni², as required.

Check the units: Nd/ni is dimensionless, so the logarithm is dimensionless; kBT is in eV; the offset is in eV. A shift of 0.37 eV is well inside the 0.56 eV half-gap, so the non-degenerate treatment is reasonable.

Where the model stops

The lab is a teaching model, not a device simulator. Four limits matter:

  • Constant gap. Eg shrinks with temperature in a real crystal (Varshni). Holding it fixed isolates the doping and ni effects.
  • Full ionisation. Every dopant is assumed ionised. At low temperature, donors and acceptors freeze out and the carrier density falls below the doping.
  • Boltzmann statistics. When EF comes within a few kBT of a band edge, the exponential tail is no longer valid; degenerate statistics and band-gap renormalisation are needed. The lab clamps the drawn level at the band edge and flags the case.
  • Midgap Ei. The true intrinsic level is shifted toward the band with the smaller effective mass. For GaAs, where Nc and Nv differ by a factor of 15, that shift is not negligible.
  • Flat bands, one material. The diagram is a homogeneous crystal with no junction, field, surface band bending, or contact. It is not a device band diagram.

A common mistake is to read “EF is in the gap” as “there are no free carriers.” The Fermi level is a chemical potential, not an occupied state; the carrier densities come from the distance to the band edges and the temperature. A second mistake is to expect doping to widen the gap: doping changes the occupation, not the band structure in this model.
