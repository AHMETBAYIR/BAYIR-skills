The unit cell is a bookkeeping device

A crystal structure is a lattice plus a basis. The cell is the smallest repeating box, but the number of atoms per cell is not the number of spheres you can count in a drawing: corners are shared by eight cells, faces by two. Rotate the cell and watch which atoms are really nearest neighbours.

Lab 01 · crystallography

Rotate an FCC cell and count what is shared

Drag to rotate. Switch between SC, BCC, FCC, diamond, and rock salt. The readout recomputes atoms per cell, coordination, packing fraction, and density from the same lattice parameter.

Predict first

FCC has four atoms per cell and coordination 12. If you double the lattice parameter, what happens to the density: double, halve, or divide by eight? Commit before you move the slider.

The dashed box is the unit cell; the shaded slice is the selected (hkl) plane. Corner atoms are shared by eight cells, face atoms by two. Drag to rotate; the sliders are the keyboard path.

Print fallback: FCC has 4 atoms per cell, Z = 12, APF = 0.740, and ρ = 4M/(NAa³).

A phase diagram is a map with a lever

At equilibrium, the phase diagram tells you which phases exist at a given composition and temperature. The lever rule then tells you how much of each: the fraction of a phase is the opposite segment of the tie line divided by the whole tie line. It is a mass balance, not a rule to memorize.

Lab 02 · phase equilibria

Move the alloy and read the tie line

Click or drag in the diagram. The tie line, the phase compositions, the lever-rule fractions, and the microstructure sketch all update together.

Predict first

At 40 wt% Sn and 200 °C, is the alloy fully liquid, in α + L, or fully solid? Then predict which phase has the larger fraction. Check your answer by dragging to that point.

Solid lines are phase boundaries; the dashed horizontal is the eutectic isotherm. The accent dot is the alloy; the accent bar is the tie line.

Print fallback: at 40 wt% Sn and 200 °C the tie line runs from about 16.8 wt% Sn (α) to about 54.6 wt% Sn (L), so fα ≈ 0.39 and fL ≈ 0.61.

The stress–strain curve is a design document

Young's modulus is the initial slope; yield is where the curve leaves linearity; the UTS is the maximum; the area under the curve is toughness. True stress and true strain correct for the changing cross-section, which is why the true curve keeps rising after the engineering curve peaks.

Lab 03 · mechanical behaviour

Read modulus, yield, UTS, and toughness from one curve

Drag the strain cursor. Switch materials and toggle true stress or the 0.2% offset construction.

Predict first

Which is larger for mild steel: the engineering toughness or the true-stress toughness? Decide before you toggle the curve, then explain the difference in one sentence.

The shaded area is energy absorbed per unit volume up to the cursor. The inset zooms the initial strain to show the 0.2% offset construction.

Print fallback: E is the initial slope, σy the first departure from linearity, σu the maximum, and toughness the area under the curve.

Diffusion is a random walk with a square-root clock

Fick's second law with a constant surface concentration gives an error-function profile. The only length in the problem is √(Dt). That is why doubling the time moves the penetration front by only √2, while a modest temperature increase can change D by orders of magnitude.

Lab 04 · kinetics

Carburise steel and watch the profile move

Change temperature, time, and surface concentration. The dashed profiles are t/4 and 4t at the same D.

Predict first

At 950 °C for 1 h, √(Dt) is about 200 µm. If you hold for 4 h instead, will the 50% depth be 400 µm, 800 µm, or 200 µm? Commit, then move the time slider.

The solid curve is the current time; the dashed curves are t/4 and 4t. The accent marker is the depth where the excess concentration has fallen by half.

Print fallback: C(x,t) = C₀ + (Cₛ − C₀)[1 − erf(x/2√(Dt))], with D = D₀ exp(−Q/RT).

A diffraction pattern is a fingerprint of the lattice

Bragg's law fixes the peak positions; the structure factor decides which peaks are allowed. For a cubic cell, d = a/√(h² + k² + l²). The sequence of allowed reflections distinguishes SC, BCC, FCC, diamond, and rock salt without a microscope.

Lab 05 · characterisation

Index a powder pattern

Switch the lattice type and the radiation. The (hkl) labels, d-spacings, and relative intensities are recalculated from the same cell.

Predict first

For an FCC metal, which reflection comes first: (100), (110), or (111)? For BCC? Write both answers down before switching the menu.

Stick heights use Cromer–Mann form factors, multiplicity, Lorentz–polarisation, and a Debye–Waller factor. Positions are exact for the stated cubic cell; absorption and texture are not modelled.

Print fallback: λ = 2d sin θ and d = a/√(h² + k² + l²). Allowed reflections: BCC h + k + l even; FCC all-odd or all-even; diamond all-odd, or all-even with h + k + l = 4n.
